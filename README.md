PHYSIOPT-X
Physics-Aware Self-Verifying Optimization and Fault-Tolerant Control Framework for Autonomous Cyber-Physical Systems
Research category: Advanced Control Engineering · Model-Based Fault Diagnosis · Adaptive Control · Constrained Optimization · Cyber-Physical Systems

Primary development environment: MATLAB / Simulink

Research level: Advanced Master's thesis, with a clearly defined pathway toward PhD research.

Central research question:
Can an autonomous control framework detect physical inconsistencies, identify plausible system faults, reconfigure a controller under explicit safety constraints, and independently verify the resulting closed-loop behavior before accepting recovery?

PART I — PROJECT FOUNDATION
1. Abstract
PHYSIOPT-X is a proposed physics-aware control and optimization framework for autonomous cyber-physical systems operating under uncertainty, component degradation, sensor faults, actuator limitations, and changing environmental conditions.

Conventional control design often assumes that the mathematical model used during controller synthesis remains sufficiently accurate during operation. In practice, the physical system may gradually or abruptly diverge from its nominal model because of changes in mass, friction, actuator effectiveness, sensor calibration, power availability, or external disturbances.

Such divergence can degrade tracking performance, increase control effort, violate operating constraints, and potentially destabilize the closed-loop system.

PHYSIOPT-X addresses this challenge through an integrated methodology combining physics-based prediction, state estimation, residual generation, fault detection, fault isolation, constrained controller optimization, supervised reconfiguration, and post-reconfiguration verification.

The proposed architecture maintains a distinction between detecting abnormal behavior and establishing its cause. It also distinguishes a controller that performs well in simulation from a controller whose safety properties have been demonstrated under stated assumptions.

The initial implementation targets a nonlinear electromechanical system, such as a DC motor with a variable load, using MATLAB and Simulink. A digital-twin model generates expected behavior, while simulated measurements and injected faults provide a controlled environment for evaluating detection latency, false alarms, diagnosis accuracy, tracking performance, constraint violations, and recovery success.

A constrained optimization layer searches for improved controller parameters. A supervisory safety layer evaluates proposed configurations against predefined operating limits, while a verification layer determines whether the system may return to normal operation, remain in degraded operation, or enter a safe-stop state.

The main research contribution is the integration of physical consistency monitoring, fault-aware optimization, and independently evaluated recovery into a single reproducible experimental framework.

Important research status: This document defines a research proposal and implementation plan. Performance improvements, experimental results, and claims of verified stability must be established by running the implementation and evaluating the resulting evidence; they are not assumed in advance.
2. Introduction
Modern autonomous systems combine physical processes, embedded computation, sensors, actuators, communication networks, and software-based decision-making.

Examples include:
- Autonomous mobile robots.
- Industrial robotic manipulators.
- Electric drives and motor-control systems.
- Autonomous vehicles.
- Smart manufacturing equipment.
- Energy-conversion systems.
- Unmanned aerial vehicles.
- Intelligent industrial automation systems.
These systems must operate despite uncertainties that cannot always be eliminated during design.

A controller may be carefully tuned for a nominal system and perform exceptionally well during initial testing. However, its behavior can deteriorate when the physical plant changes.

For example, a mobile robot may encounter a heavier payload. The additional mass changes its acceleration characteristics. A motor may lose effectiveness because of degradation. A battery may supply a lower voltage. An encoder may produce implausible measurements. Friction may increase because of mechanical wear.

A conventional controller may continue issuing commands without recognizing that the assumptions supporting its design are no longer valid.

PHYSIOPT-X investigates a more resilient alternative: a control architecture that continuously compares expected physical behavior with measured behavior and uses the resulting evidence to guide diagnosis, optimization, and recovery.
2.1 The central engineering principle
The framework begins with a simple observation:
\[
\boxed{\text{Model} \neq \text{Reality}}
\]
This expression represents the possibility of model mismatch, not a claim that the model is always incorrect.

The objective is to quantify the mismatch, determine whether it is consistent with normal uncertainty or a fault, and respond appropriately.
2.2 Research objectives
The project has eight principal objectives.
1. Develop a physics-based model of a selected electromechanical system.
2. Implement state estimation and physical consistency monitoring.
3. Detect deviations caused by defined faults and disturbances.
4. Diagnose faults using residual signatures and multiple evidence sources.
5. Optimize controller parameters under performance and safety constraints.
6. Reconfigure the controller when its existing configuration becomes unsuitable.
7. Evaluate the proposed configuration before accepting recovery.
8. Demonstrate the framework through reproducible MATLAB/Simulink experiments.
2.3 Research hypotheses
The project will test the following hypotheses.

H1 — Fault awareness: Combining physics-based residuals with temporal decision logic can distinguish selected faults from nominal disturbances more reliably than a single instantaneous threshold.

H2 — Fault-aware optimization: Reoptimizing controller parameters after an identified plant change can improve tracking performance compared with retaining the original controller, provided the optimization remains within the feasible operating region.

H3 — Supervised recovery: Separating controller proposals from supervisory acceptance can prevent an unvalidated controller configuration from being applied to the physical plant.

H4 — Graceful degradation: When safe recovery is infeasible, a supervisory controller can direct the system into a predefined degraded mode or safe-stop state.

These hypotheses are testable. None should be described as proven until the corresponding experiments have been completed.
PART II — MATHEMATICAL AND PHYSICAL MODEL
3. Why a DC motor is the right starting point
A doctoral-level framework does not need to begin with an unnecessarily complicated robot. It needs a physical system whose behavior can be modeled, whose faults can be reproduced, and whose control performance can be measured rigorously.

For PHYSIOPT-X, the initial experimental plant will be a permanent-magnet DC motor with electrical and mechanical dynamics.

The motor provides a useful test environment because it has measurable physical parameters, nonlinear operating constraints, controllable disturbances, and fault scenarios that can be introduced independently or simultaneously.

The architecture can later be extended to a mobile robot, robotic manipulator, or other cyber-physical system.

6. Fault detection versus fault diagnosis
These are different tasks.

Fault detection answers:
Has the observed behavior become sufficiently inconsistent with the expected behavior to warrant an alarm?

Fault diagnosis answers:
Which fault or combination of faults best explains the available evidence?

Fault isolation attempts to distinguish the affected component or subsystem.

Fault accommodation modifies the control strategy to tolerate the identified condition.

Fault recovery attempts to restore acceptable operation.

A strong PHYSIOPT-X implementation must not confuse these stages.

The first version should include the following reproducible fault scenarios.
Fault ID	Fault	Simulation mechanism	Expected evidence
F00	Nominal operation	No injected fault	Small residuals
F01	Increased load	Increase \(T_L\)	Speed tracking degradation
F02	Increased friction	Increase \(B\)	Increased steady-state torque requirement

F08	Communication delay	Delay measurements or commands	Phase lag and prediction error
F09	Multiple faults	Combine selected faults	Overlapping residual signatures

6.2 Fault signatures
For a more advanced diagnosis system, each fault can be associated with a vector of features:
\[
\phi_k=
\begin{bmatrix}
r_{\omega,k}\\
r_{i,k}\\
\Delta y_k\\
u_k\\
\hat{J}_k\\
\hat{B}_k\\
\hat{\eta}_{a,k}
\end{bmatrix}
\]
Here, the residuals, estimated parameters, and control signals provide complementary evidence.

For example:
- A load increase may affect mechanical acceleration while leaving the electrical circuit parameters unchanged.
- A resistance increase may affect the current response and electrical time constant.
- A sensor bias may produce disagreement between the measurement and a model prediction even when the underlying plant is healthy.
- Actuator degradation may produce lower physical response for a given requested input.
These signatures are not universally unique. Two faults may be observationally indistinguishable under a particular experiment.

Consequently, diagnosis accuracy depends on the available sensors, excitation signals, operating conditions, model quality, and fault definitions.
6.3 Advanced diagnosis options
After the initial residual detector works, the framework can be extended using:
1. Moving-window statistical tests.
2. Cumulative-sum detection.
3. Generalized likelihood-ratio methods.
4. Model-based fault isolation.
5. Recursive parameter estimation.
6. Bayesian fault classification.
7. Supervised machine-learning classifiers trained on simulated fault signatures.
8. Unknown-fault detection based on out-of-distribution behavior.
Machine learning should not replace the physics model without a reason. Its role is to complement model-based diagnosis where multiple faults generate similar residual patterns.
PART IV — CONTROL AND OPTIMIZATION
7. Baseline controller: PID
The proportional-integral-derivative controller provides the baseline against which more advanced methods can be compared.

Define the tracking error:
\[
e(t)=\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)-\omega(t)
\]
The ideal PID law is:
\[
u(t)=K_pe(t)
+K_i\int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau
+K_d\frac{de(t)}{dt}
\]
where:
- \(K_p\) controls proportional response.
- \(K_i\) compensates for persistent tracking error.
- \(K_d\) responds to error-rate changes.
The implemented control command must respect the motor's voltage limits:
\[
u_{\mathrm{applied}}
=
\operatorname{sat}
\left(
u_{\mathrm{requested}},
-V_{\max},V_{\max}
\right)
\]
This distinction matters because the requested voltage and the applied voltage are not necessarily identical.
7.1 Integral windup
When the actuator saturates, the integral term can continue accumulating error even though the actuator cannot supply the requested input.

This is called integral windup.

The implementation should therefore include an anti-windup mechanism, such as conditional integration or back-calculation.

The first executable prototype below uses a conditional-integration approach. A production-quality implementation should additionally evaluate derivative filtering, bumpless transfer, and recovery from prolonged saturation.


8. Physics-aware controller optimization
PHYSIOPT-X should not optimize tracking error alone.

A controller with a small tracking error but extreme voltage changes, excessive current, or poor robustness may be unsuitable for the actual plant.

Define the tracking error:
\[
e(t)=\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)-\omega(t)
\]
A representative performance objective is:
\[
J_{\mathrm{track}}
=
\int_0^T e^2(t)\,dt
\]
Control effort can be penalized using:
\[
J_{\mathrm{effort}}
=
\int_0^T u^2(t)\,dt
\]
Excessive changes in control can be penalized using:
\[
J_{\Delta u}
=
\sum_{k=1}^{N}
(u_k-u_{k-1})^2
\]
The combined objective becomes:
\[
\boxed{
J=
w_1J_{\mathrm{track}}
+w_2J_{\mathrm{effort}}
+w_3J_{\Delta u}
}
\]
where the weights \(w_1,w_2,w_3\) represent engineering priorities.

The objective should be appropriately normalized because tracking error, voltage, and control variation have different scales and units.
8.1 Safety constraints
The optimizer must consider limits such as:
\[
|u_k|\leq V_{\max}
\]
\[
|i_k|\leq I_{\max}
\]
\[
|\omega_k|\leq\omega_{\max}
\]
These are operating constraints, not proof of overall system safety.

A more advanced implementation can additionally impose settling-time requirements, uncertainty margins, forbidden operating regions, and limits on transient excursions.
8.2 Feasibility before optimality
The central rule is:

A lower objective value does not automatically make a candidate controller acceptable.

The candidate must first satisfy the applicable constraints and verification criteria.

If no candidate meets the acceptance criteria, the framework should reject the proposed recovery and transition to an appropriate degraded or safe-stop mode.
9. Candidate verification
After optimization, the proposed controller is tested under predefined scenarios.

The verification process should evaluate:
- Closed-loop boundedness over the tested horizon.
- Tracking error and overshoot.
- Current and voltage limits.
- Sensitivity to model mismatch.
- Behavior during fault transitions.
- Recovery transients.
- Performance under disturbances.
- Compliance with the specified acceptance criteria.
For a linear closed-loop model, eigenvalue analysis can establish asymptotic stability under the assumptions of that model.

For a nonlinear or switched system, a more complete analysis may require Lyapunov methods, invariant sets, robust stability analysis, or other appropriate techniques.

A finite simulation that does not diverge is not a mathematical proof of stability.

Likewise, checking a finite set of operating constraints during simulation does not guarantee that those constraints will hold for every possible trajectory or disturbance.

PHYSIOPT-X must distinguish empirical validation from formal verification throughout the report and software.

function physoptx_demo()
% PHYSIOPT-X v0.1
% Physics-Aware Self-Verifying Optimization and Control
%
% Demonstration plant:
%   Permanent-magnet DC motor
%
% Included:
%   1. Physical plant model
%   2. Nominal PID optimization
%   3. Load-fault injection
%   4. Physics residual monitoring
%   5. Persistent fault detection
%   6. Controller re-optimization experiment
%   7. Performance metrics and plots
%
% IMPORTANT:
% This is a simulation prototype.
% It does not constitute a formal safety proof.

clc;
close all;

rng(7, "twister");

fprintf('\n');
fprintf('============================================\n');
fprintf('           PHYSIOPT-X v0.1\n');
fprintf(' Physics-Aware Control Research Prototype\n');
fprintf('============================================\n\n');

%% 1. Simulation configuration

cfg.dt       = 0.005;
cfg.T        = 10.0;
cfg.tFault   = 5.0;
cfg.reference = 40.0;

cfg.Vmax = 12.0;
cfg.Imax = 5.0;
cfg.wMax = 150.0;

cfg.residualThreshold = 0.6;
cfg.persistenceSamples = 8;

%% 2. Nominal physical parameters

p.J  = 0.01;
p.B  = 0.001;
p.Kt = 0.05;
p.Ke = 0.05;
p.R  = 1.0;
p.L  = 0.05;

p.TL = 0.02;
p.eta = 1.0;

fprintf('Nominal motor parameters loaded.\n');

%% 3. Optimize PID parameters on the nominal plant

fprintf('\nOptimizing the nominal PID controller...\n');

initialGains = [1.0, 0.1, 0.01];

gNominal = optimizePID(initialGains, p, cfg);

fprintf('\nNominal controller gains:\n');
fprintf('Kp = %.6f\n', gNominal(1));
fprintf('Ki = %.6f\n', gNominal(2));
fprintf('Kd = %.6f\n', gNominal(3));

%% 4. Run the nominal experiment

fprintf('\nRunning nominal experiment...\n');

nominal = simulateMotor(gNominal, p, cfg, "nominal");

%% 5. Run a faulted experiment using the nominal controller
%
% At tFault, the load torque increases.
% The controller is NOT re-optimized during this run.

fprintf('Running load-fault experiment...\n');

faulted = simulateMotor(gNominal, p, cfg, "load_fault");

%% 6. Evaluate the fault detector

fprintf('\nEvaluating physics-consistency residuals...\n');

faulted.alarm = persistentDetector( ...
    faulted.residual, ...
    cfg.residualThreshold, ...
    cfg.persistenceSamples);

faultStartIndex = find( ...
    faulted.t >= cfg.tFault, 1, 'first');

alarmIndex = find( ...
    faulted.alarm & ...
    (faulted.t >= cfg.tFault), 1, 'first');

if isempty(alarmIndex)

    detectionDelay = NaN;

    fprintf(['No persistent fault alarm was generated ', ...
             'after the injected fault.\n']);

else

    detectionDelay = ...
        faulted.t(alarmIndex) - faulted.t(faultStartIndex);

    fprintf('Fault alarm generated at t = %.3f s\n', ...
        faulted.t(alarmIndex));

    fprintf('Measured detection delay = %.3f s\n', ...
        detectionDelay);

end

%% 7. Re-optimization experiment
%
% This is an offline recovery experiment.
% The faulted plant parameters are known to the experiment.
%
% A real deployment would require diagnosis or estimation
% before selecting an appropriate recovery configuration.

pFault = p;
pFault.TL = 0.045;

fprintf('\nOptimizing a candidate controller for the changed load...\n');

gRecovery = optimizePID(gNominal, pFault, cfg);

fprintf('\nCandidate recovery gains:\n');
fprintf('Kp = %.6f\n', gRecovery(1));
fprintf('Ki = %.6f\n', gRecovery(2));
fprintf('Kd = %.6f\n', gRecovery(3));

recovery = simulateMotor( ...
    gRecovery, pFault, cfg, "load_fault");

%% 8. Calculate performance metrics

mNominal = calculateMetrics(nominal, cfg);

mFault = calculateMetrics(faulted, cfg);

mRecovery = calculateMetrics(recovery, cfg);

Experiment = [
    "Nominal";
    "Fault with original controller";
    "Fault with re-optimized controller"
];

RMSE = [
    mNominal.RMSE;
    mFault.RMSE;
    mRecovery.RMSE
];

IAE = [
    mNominal.IAE;
    mFault.IAE;
    mRecovery.IAE
];

ITAE = [
    mNominal.ITAE;
    mFault.ITAE;
    mRecovery.ITAE
];

MaxAbsVoltage = [
    mNominal.MaxAbsVoltage;
    mFault.MaxAbsVoltage;
    mRecovery.MaxAbsVoltage
];

MaxAbsCurrent = [
    mNominal.MaxAbsCurrent;
    mFault.MaxAbsCurrent;
    mRecovery.MaxAbsCurrent
];

VoltageSaturationFraction = [
    mNominal.SaturationFraction;
    mFault.SaturationFraction;
    mRecovery.SaturationFraction
];

metricsTable = table( ...
    Experiment, RMSE, IAE, ITAE, ...
    MaxAbsVoltage, MaxAbsCurrent, ...
    VoltageSaturationFraction);

%% =========================================================
%  PID OPTIMIZATION
%  =========================================================

function gains = optimizePID(initialGains, p, cfg)

% Logarithmic parameterization keeps the gains positive.

initialGains = max(initialGains, 1e-6);

z0 = log(initialGains);

objective = @(z) controllerObjective(z, p, cfg);

options = optimset( ...
    'Display', 'off', ...
    'MaxIter', 100, ...
    'MaxFunEvals', 500, ...
    'TolX', 1e-4, ...
    'TolFun', 1e-4);

zBest = fminsearch(objective, z0, options);

gains = exp(zBest);

end


function Jcost = controllerObjective(z, p, cfg)

% Protect the numerical optimization from extreme values.

z = max(min(z, 5), -10);

gains = exp(z);

try

    result = simulateMotor(gains, p, cfg, "nominal");

    metrics = calculateMetrics(result, cfg);

    % Penalize excessive control effort and saturation.

    Jcost = ...
        metrics.IAE + ...
        0.01 * sum(result.u.^2) * cfg.dt + ...
        100 * metrics.SaturationFraction;

    % Penalize constraint violations.

    voltageViolation = max( ...
        0, abs(result.u) - cfg.Vmax);

    currentViolation = max( ...
        0, abs(result.i) - cfg.Imax);

    speedViolation = max( ...
        0, abs(result.omega) - cfg.wMax);

    Jcost = Jcost + ...
        1e4 * sum(voltageViolation.^2) * cfg.dt + ...
        1e4 * sum(currentViolation.^2) * cfg.dt + ...
        1e4 * sum(speedViolation.^2) * cfg.dt;

    if ~isfinite(Jcost)
        Jcost = 1e12;
    end

catch

    Jcost = 1e12;

end

end


%% =========================================================
%  DC MOTOR SIMULATION
%  =========================================================

function out = simulateMotor(gains, p, cfg, scenario)

dt = cfg.dt;

t = (0:dt:cfg.T)';

N = numel(t);

omega = zeros(N, 1);
current = zeros(N, 1);

u = zeros(N, 1);
uRequested = zeros(N, 1);

reference = cfg.reference * ones(N, 1);

residual = zeros(N, 1);

integralError = 0;

previousError = 0;

Kp = gains(1);
Ki = gains(2);
Kd = gains(3);

faultActive = false;

for k = 1:N-1

    tk = t(k);

    % -----------------------------------------------------
    % Fault injection
    % -----------------------------------------------------

    TL = p.TL;

    eta = p.eta;

    if scenario == "load_fault" && tk >= cfg.tFault

        TL = 0.045;

        faultActive = true;

    end

    % -----------------------------------------------------
    % Measured speed
    % -----------------------------------------------------

    measuredOmega = omega(k);

    error = reference(k) - measuredOmega;

    derivativeError = (error - previousError) / dt;

    % -----------------------------------------------------
    % PID controller with conditional integration
    % -----------------------------------------------------

    uTrial = ...
        Kp * error + ...
        Ki * integralError + ...
        Kd * derivativeError;

    uTrial = max(min(uTrial, cfg.Vmax), -cfg.Vmax);

    % Conditional integration:
    % Do not integrate further into saturation.

    integrateAllowed = ...
        (abs(uTrial) < cfg.Vmax) || ...
        (uTrial >= cfg.Vmax && error < 0) || ...
        (uTrial <= -cfg.Vmax && error > 0);

    if integrateAllowed

        integralError = integralError + error * dt;

    end

    % Recalculate after updating the integral state.

    uRequested(k) = ...
        Kp * error + ...
        Ki * integralError + ...
        Kd * derivativeError;

    u(k) = max(min(uRequested(k), cfg.Vmax), -cfg.Vmax);

    % -----------------------------------------------------
    % Nominal-model prediction
    %
    % The reference model retains nominal load and
    % nominal actuator effectiveness.
    % -----------------------------------------------------

    omegaDotModel = ...
        (p.Kt * current(k) - ...
         p.B * omega(k) - p.TL) / p.J;

    omegaPredicted = omega(k) + omegaDotModel * dt;

    % -----------------------------------------------------
    % Actual physical plant
    % -----------------------------------------------------

    omegaDot = ...
        (p.Kt * eta * current(k) - ...
         p.B * omega(k) - TL) / p.J;

    currentDot = ...
        (eta * u(k) - ...
         p.R * current(k) - ...
         p.Ke * omega(k)) / p.L;

    % -----------------------------------------------------
    % Forward Euler integration
    % -----------------------------------------------------

    omega(k+1) = omega(k) + omegaDot * dt;

    current(k+1) = current(k) + currentDot * dt;

    % -----------------------------------------------------
    % Physics residual
    %
    % The residual compares the measured acceleration
    % with the nominal model's predicted acceleration.
    % -----------------------------------------------------

    residual(k) = ...
        (omega(k+1) - omega(k)) / dt - omegaDotModel;

    previousError = error;

end

u(N) = u(N-1);

uRequested(N) = uRequested(N-1);

residual(N) = residual(N-1);

out.t = t;

out.omega = omega;

out.i = current;

out.u = u;

out.uRequested = uRequested;

out.reference = reference;

out.residual = residual;

out.faultActive = faultActive;

end


%% =========================================================
%  PERSISTENT-THRESHOLD FAULT DETECTOR
%  =========================================================

function alarm = persistentDetector(residual, threshold, Npersist)

N = numel(residual);

alarm = false(N, 1);

count = 0;

for k = 1:N

    if abs(residual(k)) > threshold

        count = count + 1;

    else

        count = 0;

    end

    if count >= Npersist

        alarm(k) = true;

    end

end

end


%% =========================================================
%  PERFORMANCE METRICS
%  =========================================================

function m = calculateMetrics(out, cfg)

t = out.t;

e = out.reference - out.omega;

dt = cfg.dt;

m.RMSE = sqrt(mean(e.^2));

m.IAE = sum(abs(e)) * dt;

m.ITAE = sum(t .* abs(e)) * dt;

m.MaxAbsVoltage = max(abs(out.u));

m.MaxAbsCurrent = max(abs(out.i));

m.SaturationFraction = mean( ...
    abs(out.u) >= cfg.Vmax - 1e-9);

end

13. Understanding the implementation
The code is deliberately organized around the engineering logic of PHYSIOPT-X.
Stage 1 — Establish a physical baseline
The motor begins with known nominal parameters.

The simulation computes its current and speed using the electrical and mechanical differential equations.

This establishes a repeatable baseline against which later experiments can be compared.
Stage 2 — Optimize the nominal controller
The function optimizePID uses MATLAB's fminsearch to search for PID gains that reduce the objective function.

The objective penalizes tracking error, control effort, actuator saturation, and selected constraint violations.

fminsearch is a derivative-free local optimization method. It does not guarantee a global optimum, and the logarithmic parameterization in this prototype does not impose formal upper bounds on the gains.

The implementation therefore provides an initial optimization experiment rather than a globally optimal or formally certified controller.
Stage 3 — Introduce a physical fault
At the specified fault time, the load torque changes from:
\[
T_{L,0}=0.02\ \text{N·m}
\]
to:
\[
T_{L,1}=0.045\ \text{N·m}
\]
The original controller continues operating without being retuned.

This allows us to measure how the existing controller responds to a change in the plant.
Stage 4 — Compare prediction with physical behavior
The nominal model predicts the angular acceleration:
\[
\dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{model}}
=
\frac{K_t i-B\omega-T_{L,0}}{J}
\]
The simulation calculates the actual angular acceleration and compares the two:
\[
\boxed{
r_k=
\frac{\omega_{k+1}-\omega_k}{\Delta t}
-\dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{model},k}
}
\]
An increase in load torque creates a discrepancy between the nominal prediction and the actual plant acceleration.

The residual detector evaluates whether that discrepancy persists beyond the configured threshold.

A critical limitation: this prototype uses the simulated plant's current state to calculate the residual. In a real application, the model would need to obtain equivalent quantities through actual sensors, a state estimator, or another justified estimation method. The implementation also uses a known load-fault scenario, rather than estimating an unknown load automatically.

These simplifications are acceptable for a first simulation experiment, but they must be removed or explicitly addressed before claiming a hardware-ready implementation.
Stage 5 — Optimize a recovery candidate
The code optimizes a new PID controller for the altered load.

This is an offline experiment: the faulted plant parameters are supplied directly to the optimizer.

It demonstrates whether a different controller configuration can improve performance in the selected scenario. It does not yet demonstrate autonomous diagnosis of an unknown fault followed by automatic recovery.
Stage 6 — Screen the candidate
The code checks whether the candidate:
- Respects the specified voltage limit.
- Respects the specified current limit.
- Respects the specified speed limit.
- Produces finite numerical values.
- Improves the RMSE relative to the faulted experiment with the original controller.
If all conditions pass, the candidate passes the implemented screening checks.

This is a deliberately limited verification layer. It does not prove stability for all initial conditions, robustness to all disturbances, or satisfaction of constraints between simulation samples.

A more advanced version must evaluate the candidate using independent test scenarios and a stronger verification method.
PART VI — EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN AND RESULTS
14. What results should PHYSIOPT-X produce?
This is one of the most important sections of the entire project.

A research project should not simply show that the simulation runs. It should establish measurable questions, define the experiments needed to answer them, and report the results honestly.

The MATLAB prototype generates initial performance metrics and plots. Its actual numerical results must be obtained by executing the code in MATLAB.

Until that has been done, we must distinguish expected behavior, measured results, and research hypotheses.
14.1 Primary performance metrics
Metric	Mathematical definition	What it measures
RMSE	\(\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum e_k^2}\)	Overall tracking error
IAE	(\int_0^T	e(t)
ISE	\(\int_0^T e^2(t)\,dt\)	Penalizes large errors
ITAE	(\int_0^T t	e(t)
Overshoot	Maximum excursion above the target	Transient tracking quality
Settling time	Time to remain within a specified error band	Speed of convergence
Control effort	\(\int_0^T u^2(t)\,dt\)	Relative control activity
Saturation fraction	Fraction of samples at actuator limits	How often the actuator is saturated
Detection latency	Alarm time minus fault onset	How quickly a fault is detected
False-alarm rate	False alarms per unit time or test	Unnecessary fault warnings
Diagnosis accuracy	Correct diagnoses divided by evaluated diagnoses	Fault classification performance
Recovery time	Time from recovery initiation to accepted recovery	Speed of the recovery process
Constraint violations	Number and magnitude of limit violations	Compliance with specified limits


Not all metrics are included in version 0.1. The current script calculates RMSE, IAE, ITAE, maximum voltage, maximum current, and saturation fraction. It also measures detection delay when a persistent alarm occurs.

Overshoot, settling time, false-alarm rate, diagnosis accuracy, and recovery time should be added in later versions.
14.2 Why one metric is insufficient
Consider two controllers.

Controller A might have a lower RMSE but frequently saturate the actuator.

Controller B might have a slightly higher RMSE while remaining within a more comfortable operating region.

Depending on the application, Controller B may be preferable.

Similarly, a detector that identifies every fault but generates hundreds of false alarms is not necessarily useful.

PHYSIOPT-X must therefore report multiple complementary metrics instead of presenting one attractive number as proof of overall superiority.

- Tracking degradation.
- Residual magnitude.
